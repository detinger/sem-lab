### Theory and *illustrative* hypotheses

In ACSI-type models, customer expectations (**CE**) and perceived quality (**PQ**) shape perceived
value (**PV**, quality relative to price); the three drive customer satisfaction (**SAT**), and
satisfaction drives loyalty (**LOY**). The original ACSI also models customer complaints, which
we leave out.

| | Hypothesis (illustrative) | | Hypothesis (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | CE → PQ (+) | H5 | PQ → SAT (+) |
| H2 | CE → PV (+) | H6 | PV → SAT (+) |
| H3 | PQ → PV (+) | H7 | SAT → LOY (+) |
| H4 | CE → SAT (+) | | |

The data are synthetic and cross-sectional: results illustrate the method, they say nothing
about real customers, and the arrows are model assumptions, not demonstrated causal effects.

## 8. A design choice: reflective (Mode A) or composite (Mode B) perceived quality?

Whether a block is reflective or composite is a **theoretical** decision, made before looking at
the data. A *reflective* construct is a common cause of its items (interchangeable items; the
construct is estimated with Mode A weights, which follow the correlations of each item with the
construct proxy). A *composite* construct is formed by its items (each item contributes a distinct
facet; Mode B weights are multiple-regression weights). In the ACSI tradition, quality and
satisfaction items are usually treated as reflective. To see what the choice changes, we
re-estimate the model with PQ specified as a composite (Mode B), without bootstrap.

%% code
import copy

model_b = copy.deepcopy(model)
for c in model_b["constructs"]:
    if c["name"] == "PQ":
        c["measurement"] = "composite-formative"
analysis_b = {k: v for k, v in analysis.items() if k not in ("bootstrap", "seed", "ipma")}
out_b = semlab.estimate(model_b, analysis_b, semlab.build_analysis_data(model_b, columns, data_rows, "listwise"))
assert out_b["ok"], out_b.get("error")
rep_b = pls_report(out_b["result"], model_b, semlab)

w_a = rep["weights"].query("construct == 'PQ'").set_index("indicator")["weight"]
w_b = rep_b["weights"].query("construct == 'PQ'").set_index("indicator")["weight"]
show(pd.DataFrame({"Mode A weight": w_a, "Mode B weight": w_b}))
cmp = paths.set_index("path")[["est"]].rename(columns={"est": "PQ reflective (Mode A)"})
cmp["PQ composite (Mode B)"] = rep_b["paths"].set_index("path")["est"]
cmp["difference"] = cmp["PQ composite (Mode B)"] - cmp["PQ reflective (Mode A)"]
show(cmp)
print("Quality criteria reported for PQ under Mode B:")
show(rep_b["quality"].loc[["PQ"]])

%% markdown
**Interpretation.** With highly correlated, similar items, Mode A and Mode B give similar
weights and almost the same paths here; Mode B weights are less stable (they are regression
weights on collinear items) and can even become negative with real data. The important
difference is what you may report: for a composite (Mode B) block, α, ρA, ρC and AVE are **not
applicable** (SEM Lab reports them as missing with a reason), and validity is argued through
the weights, their significance and the indicator VIFs instead. Choose the specification from
theory, report it, and never switch modes to "improve" results.

## 9. Importance–performance map analysis (IPMA)

IPMA adds a second dimension to the structural results:

- **Importance** of a construct for the target = its *unstandardised* total effect on the
  target, after every indicator has been rescaled to 0–100 with its declared endpoints
  (here 1 and 10: score = (x − 1) / 9 × 100).
- **Performance** = the mean of the construct's 0–100 score (a weighted average of its
  rescaled indicators).

A construct with high importance but low performance is an improvement priority: raising its
performance by one point would raise loyalty more than for the other constructs, and there is
more room to improve. The full convention is stored with the result
(`result["ipma"]["convention"]`).

%% code
ipma_c = pd.DataFrame(semlab.results_tables(result, model)["ipmaConstructs"])
ipma_c = ipma_c[["construct", "importance", "performance", "predecessor"]]
show(ipma_c)
FIGURES["ipma_loyalty.png"] = ipma_plot(ipma_c, "LOY")
plt.show()

%% markdown
**Interpretation.** Satisfaction is by far the most important driver of loyalty (it is the only
direct predictor). Among the antecedents, perceived quality has the highest importance and also
the highest performance; expectations combine a moderate importance with a below-average
performance; **perceived value has the lowest performance** (about 55 of 100) at a smaller but
positive importance. In a managerial reading, raising value-for-money perceptions or the
lower-performing aspects of expectations are candidate priorities, while quality is already a
strength. Importance values are unstandardised effects: an importance of 0.34 for PQ means
that a 1-point increase of PQ's performance goes with a 0.34-point increase of LOY's
performance, other things equal — an association in this model, not a guaranteed lever.

%% markdown
**Interpretation.** For satisfaction, perceived quality is by far the most important driver
(0.60), followed by expectations (0.38, mostly through quality) and value (0.29). The performance values are the same as before (performance does not
depend on the target); only importance changes. PV again combines a clear importance with the
lowest performance. Caveats: IPMA inherits every limitation of the underlying model (here a
synthetic, cross-sectional data set), importance is a model-based total effect, and the
performance scale depends on the declared endpoints being the true scale endpoints.

## 11. Summary and questions

- Five reflective constructs on 10-point items pass the reliability, AVE and HTMT checks; CE is
  the weakest block.
- All seven hypothesised paths are positive with intervals excluding zero; quality and value
  drive satisfaction, satisfaction drives loyalty.
- IPMA for loyalty: SAT is the dominant driver; PV has the lowest performance; PQ is important
  and already performs well.
- Specifying PQ as a composite hardly changes the paths here, but it changes which quality
  criteria are meaningful.

**Questions**

1. Why do CE, PQ and PV have importance for loyalty even though none of them has a direct path
   to LOY?
2. What would happen to the performance values if the model declared the scale as 0–10 instead
   of 1–10? And to the importance values?
3. Why may you not report AVE for PQ when it is specified as a composite?
4. PQ has high importance *and* high performance. Is it a priority? Argue both ways.
